Tong et al.27 introduced problem schema with a hierarchical exercise graph to KT. The problem
schema clustered similar exercises into the same group to incorporate the question–question re-
lations. Pandey et al.15 calculated the exercise–exercise relations based on the performance data and
the exercise text, and incorporated it into a transformer model for relation‐aware KT. All of these
methods exploit the relation information in various graphs and introduce the distinctive in-
formation of questions into the KT task. However, they ignore the KS information of the skills in
the domain. Similarly to our work, Nakagawa et al.25 and Chen et al.20 incorporated the KS
information into the KT procedure. Chen et al.'s work assumes that the KS is already given by
experts, and models the prerequisite as an ordering pair. In this way, the mastery of related skills is
constrained by referring to the KS. Nakagawa et al. used the KS information and a graph neural
network (GNN) to update the learners' hidden knowledge states. Unlike these earlier works, our
method learns the KS from learner response data and integrates it with the Q‐matrix to build a KS‐
enhanced relation graph. Leveraging the Metapath2Vec method, we learn the dense embeddings of
all nodes, which incorporate the multihop question–question, question–skill, and skill–skill rela-
tions in the domain. The different types of models are compared in Table 1.

Knowledge structure discovery
Knowledge structure discovery facilitates the intelligence of online learning systems by pro-
viding personalized learning paths and services to accommodate the needs of different learners.
Automating this process has been attempted in several studies. Most of the existing KS‐
discovery methods identify the latent skills required for answering the questions, and find the
similarities among the questions in the domain for clustering the potential KS. The technique
of Lindsey et al.43 discovers a skill set that learners use for problem solving. This technique
assumes a monotonic improvement of learner performance along a sequence of questions
requiring the same skill. Pan et al.23 automatically inferred the prerequisite relation between
knowledge concepts in MOOCs using natural language processing techniques. Mussack et al.44
discovered the problem similarity by combing the problem features and learner behaviors.
Rihák et al.45 and Nazaretsky et al.46 proposed various measurement indices that cluster the
education items from performance data. Their methods assume that learner performances are
similar on items requiring the same skills. However, in these studies, clusters of questions
requiring the same latent skills are regarded as the KS in the domain; in contrast, our KS is
constructed from the prerequisite relations among explicit skills.
Wang et al.47 proposed a latent‐variable selection method with regularization for cognitive
diagnostics, which learns the skill hierarchies from learner response data. Using a DKT model,
Zhang et al.24 discovered the topological order of skills from learners' exercise performance.
Like our group, they intuited that the KS is difficult to directly extract from response data, but
this difficulty can be bridged by ordering the learners' mastery of skills. Hence, in this paper, we
discover the prerequisite skill pairs in the KS by ordering the learners' performances in
question‐solving (i.e., their mastery of the underlying skills).

PROPOSED METHOD
This section introduces the proposed KSGKT framework for KT, as shown in Figure 2. The
framework proceeds the KT task using five modules: knowledge structure discovery, graph‐
based embedding learning, convolutional question representation, attention mechanism, and
learner knowledge state evolution. Before conducting the embedding learning, we must build
the KS‐enhanced question–skill graph by integrating the KS inferred from the learner response
data into the question–skill graph. Based on the built KS‐enhanced question–skill graph, the
dense embeddings of all skill and question nodes are obtained through the graph embedding
learning method Methpath2Vec. To incorporate more distinctive information of the questions,
the cognitive question difficulty at both the question and skill levels are also inferred from the
learning histories of individual learners. A convolutional representation method is then pro-
posed to fuse the question and skill embeddings with the cognitive question difficulty. It
considers each separate factor and the interactions between each pair of factors, thus obtaining
the comprehensive representations of questions (Figure 3). These representations are fed to the
attentive KT network for predicting learner performance (see Figure 4, for the KT procedure).
To capture the long‐term dependencies in the exercising sequence, different attention me-
chanisms are applied. For reference, the algorithm for our proposed method is shown in
Algorithm 1.

EXPERIMENTAL SETTINGS
In a series of experimental tasks, we evaluated the proposed KSGKT model on three public real‐
world data sets. This section describes the experimental settings (aims, data sets, comparison
baselines, setup and implementation, and evaluation metrics). The detailed experimental re-
sults and model analysis are presented in the next section.
5.1
|
Experimental aims
Our experiments aimed to answer the following questions:
1. Based on the knowledge proficiency inferred from the learners' exercise histories, how well
does KSGKT predict the learners' performance on new questions? (See Section 6.1).
2. How does embedding learning on the KS‐enhanced graph affect the performance of the
proposed model? (See Section 6.2.1).
3. Does the question embedding learned on the graph provides meaningful information? (See
Section 6.2.2).
4. How well do the eight methods infer the KS from data? Is the inferred KS explainable? (See
Section 6.2.3 and 6.2.4).
5. How effective is the convolutional question representation of the KT task? (See
Section 6.2.5).
6. How effective is the attention mechanism in the proposed method? What is the effect of
changing the attention‐calculating method? (See Section 6.2.6).

Data sets
Experiments were performed on three well‐established data sets, namely, Assist0910,* As-
sist1213,† and EdNet‡. All data sets are public real‐world data sets containing the temporal
interaction records between learners and real computer‐aided tutoring systems. These data sets
incorporate rich information on the learners and questions, along with their interactions over
time; hence, they are very suitable for long‐term dynamic KT studies in large‐scale scenarios.
Assist0910: This data set was collected from 2009 to 2010 using the ASSISTment system.54 It
contains the interactions of 3002 learners and 17,705 items associated with 123 skills, forming
277,540 interaction records.
Assist1213: This data set, which updates Assist0910, was collected over the whole year of
2012. In this data set, 22,339 learners in the system attempted 52,825 mathematics questions
requiring 265 skills. This data set contains the largest number of learners, questions, and
interaction entries (nearly 2.7 million). It is a one‐skill‐per‐question data set, meaning that each
question requires one skill.
EdNet: The EdNet data set is newly available to the public.55 Following [12], we randomly
selected 5000 students who answered 12,372 questions requiring 188 skills, thus obtaining
347,866 interaction logs. This data set contains the minimum number of attempted items per
learner (69.57) but the largest number of skills per item among the three data sets.
As done in existing studies,9,12 we eliminated noise by deleting users with fewer than 10
interaction entries and questions with “not‐a number” (NaN) skills from the three data sets.
