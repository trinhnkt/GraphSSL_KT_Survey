1. Introduction

    The rapid expansion of online learning platforms has generated an immense volume of learner behavior data, opening up
new opportunities for the development of Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) (Wang, Lai and Huang, 2024). Effectively managing
and analyzing this data, as well as accurately assessing a learner’s knowledge state, continues to pose significant challenges for
ITS. Knowledge Tracing (KT), which draws on educational theory and leverages data-driven methodologies, is designed to model
cognitive progression and dynamically infer learners’ knowledge states from historical learning data (Abdelrahman, Wang, & Nunes,
2023; Qian, Zheng, Wang, & Li, 2024). It provides valuable insights and practical tools for making decisions and managing in
education, serving as a powerful driver for personalized learning (Wang & Sahebi, 2023).
    Early KT models based on Hidden Markov Models treat each knowledge concept as an independent variable (Falakmasir, Pardos,
Gordon, & Brusilovsky, 2013), which can be considered a pointwise model. We refer to it as Pointwise Knowledge Tracing (POI-
KT), as shown in Fig. 1(a). Although the approaches are relatively straightforward to implement, they neglect the interdependencies
between knowledge concepts, often leading to limited effectiveness in applications (Liu, Hu, Bu, & Yu, 2022). The emergence of deep
learning technologies motivates researchers to employ more advanced neural network models to enhance the predictive abilities of
KT (Yang, Yu, Ma, Zhang, & Li, 2022). Whether the backbone network is based on recurrent neural networks (Piech et al., 2015) or

self-attention networks (Ghosh, Heffernan, & Lan, 2020), the hidden layers of these models inherently consider the latent connections
between knowledge concepts during parameter optimization, enabling them to predict future responses effectively. They do not
explicitly depict the associations among knowledge concepts, relying instead on internal parameters for implicit learning, which
we classify as Implicit Knowledge Tracing (IMP-KT), as shown in Fig. 1(b). Although they show some improvement in predictive
accuracy, their flexibility is limited in contexts with complex knowledge structures. After that, researchers have applied graph
models to represent the relations between knowledge concepts (Huang et al., 2024), significantly enhancing predictive performance
in specific contexts. We refer to these models as Explicit Knowledge Tracing (EXP-KT), as depicted in Fig. 1(c), which effectively
captures intricate dependencies among concepts, thereby improving the accuracy of learner behavior predictions.
    Current research on modeling learners’ knowledge structures has predominantly focused on single-level, planar relations between
knowledge concepts, without fully considering the complexity of the hierarchical organization and multi-level collaboration of
knowledge concepts revealed by cognitive architecture theory (Sweller, Van Merrienboer, & Paas, 1998). For example, when a
learner answers a question related to ‘‘finding a common denominator’’, they not only need to understand this concept but also
master the higher-level concept of the ‘‘basic properties of fractions’’. Specifically, in order to solve this problem, the learner must
first understand the ‘‘basic properties of fractions’’ in order to identify that the question is testing ‘‘finding a common denominator’’
rather than ‘‘simplifying a fraction’’ and then apply the relevant knowledge to answer correctly. Therefore, multi-level modeling of
knowledge concepts is crucial for accurately assessing learners’ knowledge structures.
    Guided by cognitive architecture theory (Sweller et al., 1998), learners’ cognitive state modeling with the hierarchical structure
of knowledge concepts involves tackling two key challenges. (1) The difference in the level of abstraction of various knowledge
concepts makes it a significant challenge to clearly define the hierarchical structure of these concepts within a unified framework.
(2) Different levels of knowledge have varying impacts on learner responses, making it necessary to assess the influence weight of
each level. However, existing knowledge tracing (KT) methods typically focus on modeling a single level, neglecting the synergistic
effects between different levels, which in turn affects the model’s prediction accuracy and generalization ability. Addressing these
challenges is vital for developing effective models that accurately capture and utilize the hierarchical nature of knowledge concepts.
    In this work, we propose the Hierarchical Knowledge Tracing (HKT) model, which explicitly incorporates the hierarchical
structure of knowledge concepts. It provides a more nuanced understanding of learner performance, distinguishing it from existing
methods, as depicted in Fig. 1(d). For (1), we introduce a Knowledge Hierarchy Tree (KHT) based on expert prior knowledge, which
models the vertical hierarchical relationships between knowledge concepts and establishes a top-down knowledge organization
framework. Meanwhile, we construct a Knowledge Association Graph (KAG) using learning sequence information to represent
interrelations between concepts at the same hierarchical level. Subsequently, we construct hierarchical embedding representations
that not only indicate the level of each knowledge concept but also capture the distances between levels. These embeddings are
further refined through the application of a cross-graph, hierarchical contrastive learning strategy. For (2), we propose weight
allocation networks that can dynamically assess the impact of different levels of knowledge concepts on prediction results. By
adaptively assigning different levels of weights based on the relevance of the prediction task, the model can provide a comprehensive
evaluation of the learner’s responses. Experimental results based on real-world datasets show a significant improvement in predictive
performance. Furthermore, our approach exhibits strong interpretability. The explicit modeling of knowledge concepts using KHT
and KAG provides an interpretable and structured knowledge framework that aligns with human cognition. The dynamic weight

The principal contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

     1. We combine knowledge tracing with a hierarchical knowledge structure model, moving beyond traditional single-level flat
        models to establish a multi-level, hierarchical framework.
     2. We introduce the KHT and KAG, which model the relations between knowledge concepts across different levels and within

     3. We design the weight allocation networks that dynamically assign weights to knowledge concepts at different levels,

     4. Experiments on real-world datasets show that our model outperforms other methods, achieving superior predictive perfor-

2. Related work

2.1. Knowledge tracing

    Knowledge Tracing is a fundamental task in intelligent tutoring systems, aiming to dynamically infer a learner’s knowledge state
based on their learning interactions (Jiang, Wei, Zhang, & Zhang, 2024; Wang & Sahebi, 2023; Zhao, Xia, Jiang, & He, 2023).
With the rapid accumulation of educational data and advancements in deep learning techniques, KT methods have evolved from
traditional statistical models to sophisticated deep learning-based approaches (Wang et al., 2023). Based on different strategies for
modeling knowledge concepts, We categorize KT methods into three main types: Pointwise Knowledge Tracing (POI-KT), Implicit
Knowledge Tracing (IMP-KT), and Explicit Knowledge Tracing with relations Modeling (EXP-KT).
    POI-KT was a key focus in the early development of KT methods, with Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) (Corbett & Anderson,
1994) serving as a notable example. These approaches treat each knowledge concept as an independent variable and employ a
Bayesian probabilistic framework to update a learner’s mastery of specific knowledge over time (Bulut, Shin, Yildirim-Erbasli,
Gorgun, & Pardos, 2023). While POI-KT methods are both simple and easy to interpret, they rest on the assumption that knowledge
concepts are entirely independent. This simplification disregards the intricate relations that exist within complex knowledge
networks, thereby limiting their capacity to handle interconnected knowledge structures effectively. Consequently, their predictive
accuracy tends to be suboptimal in scenarios involving more sophisticated knowledge interdependencies.
    With the rapid advancement of deep learning technologies, researchers have developed increasingly sophisticated models to
capture the intricate relations between learners’ behaviors and underlying knowledge concepts (Casalino et al., 2021). Within
the framework of IMP-KT, models such as DKT (Piech et al., 2015) and AKT (Ghosh et al., 2020) have emerged as exemplary
approaches. DKT employs Recurrent Neural Networks (Zaremba, 2014) to model learners’ sequential interactions, effectively cap-
turing personalized learning trajectories. However, it relies on implicit network architectures to infer associations among knowledge
concepts, lacking explicit modeling of these relations, which can constrain its performance. AKT (Ghosh et al., 2020) utilizes
multi-layer attention mechanisms (Waswani et al., 2017) to model the mastery and forgetting dynamics of knowledge concepts,
thereby enhancing the predictive performance of knowledge tracing. Despite these implicit association modeling techniques leverage
deep learning to achieve performance enhancements, the absence of clear representations of knowledge relations restricts their
effectiveness, particularly in educational environments characterized by complex hierarchical knowledge structures.
    Additionally, researchers have introduced EXP-KT methods that leverage graph neural networks to explicitly model relations
between knowledge concepts. One prominent example is GKT (Nakagawa, Iwasawa, & Matsuo, 2019), which models questions and
knowledge concepts as nodes and employs graph neural networks to capture their relations. While GKT models knowledge relations
at a single level, it struggles to represent the multi-level hierarchical structures found in real-world knowledge. Most existing EXP-KT
methods focus primarily on flat or same-level relations, which limits their effectiveness in scenarios involving complex knowledge
hierarchies.

2.2. Graph representation learning

    Representation learning seeks to extract meaningful feature representations from raw data through automated methods, enabling
their application across a range of downstream tasks (Bengio, Courville, & Vincent, 2013). As a significant branch of representation
learning, Graph Representation Learning (GRL) (Hamilton, 2020) focuses on embedding nodes, edges, and their attributes from
graph structures into a vector space, while preserving the graph’s topology and semantic information (Chen, Wang, Wang, & Kuo,
2020; Li, Jian et al., 2024). These embeddings not only capture the relations between nodes effectively but also support various
tasks such as classification, clustering, and link prediction.
    Early approaches to graph representation learning predominantly relied on random walk techniques and matrix factorization.
Notable examples include DeepWalk (Perozzi, Al-Rfou, & Skiena, 2014) and node2vec (Grover & Leskovec, 2016), which simulate
random walks to generate sequences of nodes and employ the Skip-gram model to learn node embeddings. These methods effectively
capture structural information by maximizing local neighborhood similarity. Furthermore, techniques such as negative sampling
and noise contrastive estimation (NCE) (Gutmann & Hyvärinen, 2010) are widely adopted to optimize embeddings, improving their
robustness and discriminative power.

    With the development of deep learning, GCL has become a key direction in graph representation learning (Khoshraftar & An,
2024). GCL focuses on learning robust and semantically rich node embeddings by constructing positive and negative sample pairs and
employing contrastive loss functions, such as InfoNCE loss (Oord, Li, & Vinyals, 2018). Notable approaches, such as GraphCL (You
et al., 2020) and Deep Graph Infomax (Veličković et al., 2018), enhance the performance and generalization of graph representation
learning through diverse data augmentation strategies and contrastive objectives (Zhang, Niu, Ma, & Zhang, 2025).
    In KT tasks, GRL has been employed to model the complex relations between knowledge concepts (Tong et al., 2020), thereby
improving the accuracy of inferring learners’ knowledge states. Recently, KT methods based on graph contrastive learning have
gained prominence, utilizing contrastive learning strategies to further enhance embedding quality and model generalization (Wang,
Ma, Zhao and Li, 2024). However, most existing approaches focus primarily on flattened graph structures, neglecting the hierarchical
organization of knowledge concepts, which restricts their effectiveness in scenarios with complex knowledge structures.


3. Preliminaries


    This section presents the preliminary knowledge, including the key definitions and notations required throughout this paper.
    Knowledge tracing (KT) is a critical technique in the development of intelligent education systems, enabling the dynamic
evaluation and prediction of a learner’s mastery of knowledge over time. Let the set of learners be denoted as  = {𝑠1 , 𝑠2 , … , 𝑠𝑍 },
where || = 𝑍, and the set of questions as  = {𝑞1 , 𝑞2 , … , 𝑞𝑀 }, where || = 𝑀. The primary goal of knowledge tracing is to model
a learner’s historical interaction sequence 𝑠 = {𝑥𝑠1 , 𝑥𝑠2 , … , 𝑥𝑠𝑡 }, in order to predict their performance on a future question at the next
time step 𝑡 + 1. Each interaction 𝑥𝑢𝑡 is composed of a question 𝑞𝑡 and the corresponding learner’s response 𝑟𝑡 , where 𝑟𝑡 = 1 indicates a
correct answer and 𝑟𝑡 = 0 otherwise. The objective is to estimate the probability that a learner will correctly answer a given question
𝑞𝑡+1 at time 𝑡 + 1, conditioned on their historical interactions. The probability is formally expressed as: 𝑝(𝑎𝑡+1 = 1 ∣ 𝑠 , 𝑞𝑡+1 ).
    A knowledge concept is denoted as 𝑐 ∈ , where  = {𝑐1 , 𝑐2 , … , 𝑐𝑁 } represents a set of 𝑁 distinct knowledge concepts. In
general, these knowledge concepts are structured hierarchically to represent relations between them. This hierarchical structure can
be formally modeled as a Knowledge Hierarchy Tree  = (, , ), where  represents the set of nodes, with each node 𝑣 ∈ 
uniquely corresponding to a knowledge concept in ,  represents the set of edges, with each edge 𝑒𝑢𝑣 ∈  connecting two nodes 𝑢
and 𝑣,  represents the levels of the hierarchy, where each level 𝑖 ∈  specifies the depth of nodes within the tree. To quantify the
hierarchical structure, a mapping 𝜙 ∶  →  is defined, such that 𝜙(𝑣) provides the hierarchical level of node 𝑣. By convention, the
root node of the tree is assigned a level of 1.
    To capture relation between knowledge concepts at the same hierarchical level, we introduce the Knowledge Association Graph.
For the 𝑘th level of the hierarchy, the graph is defined as 𝑘 = ( 𝑘 ,  𝑘 ), where  𝑘 = {𝑣 ∈  ∣ 𝜙(𝑣) = 𝑘} is the set of nodes at
the 𝑘th level,  𝑘 is the set of edges representing relations between these nodes. The edges  𝑘 are constructed based on semantic
dependencies, derived from the co-occurrence patterns of knowledge concepts in learners’ response sequences. By analyzing how
knowledge concepts appear together in responses, we identify and quantify their relations, which are then represented as edges in
the graph. The Knowledge Association Graph consists of multiple subgraphs, each corresponding to a specific hierarchical level. The
complete graph is expressed as  = {1 , 2 , … , 𝐼 }, where 𝑘 is the subgraph for the 𝑘th level, and 𝐼 is the total number of levels.

4. Methodology


    As shown in Fig. 2, HKT begins by modeling the hierarchical relations within the KHT and the associative relations between
knowledge concepts in the KAG. It then employs a hierarchy-oriented contrastive learning strategy to generate knowledge concept
embeddings that effectively capture hierarchical information. Finally, a weight allocation network is used to accurately assess the
influence of knowledge concepts at different levels on prediction outcomes and to allocate their weights in a rational manner for
the final prediction task.

4.6. Optimization

   In this work, we employed a phased training strategy. Initially, the embeddings of KHT and KAG are optimized using graph
reconstruction and cross-graph hierarchical contrastive learning methods. These optimized embeddings are then incorporated into
the weight allocation networks, which are jointly optimized with both the weight allocation loss 𝑤 and the prediction loss 𝑝 . The
entire process is optimized using the Adam optimizer and the algorithm is shown in Algorithms 1 and 2.

4.7. Complexity analysis

   The complexity of the proposed HKT model consists of two main components: (1) the computation of knowledge concept
representations via KHT and KAG, and (2) the performance prediction through the weight allocation module. Specifically, for the
knowledge concept representation part, the complexity is 𝑂(𝑙 ⋅ 𝑘 ⋅ |𝐺|), where 𝑙 represents the number of encoding layers, 𝑘 is the
number of negative samples sampled in hierarchical contrastive learning, and |𝐺| is the number of nodes in the constructed KHT
or KAG. Since both have the same number of nodes, no separate distinction is made. For the weight allocation networks module,
the complexity is 𝑂(𝑇 ), where 𝑇 is the length of the historical exercise records. Therefore, the overall time complexity of the HKT
model is 𝑂(𝑙 ⋅ 𝑘 ⋅ |𝐺|) + 𝑂(𝑇 ), which is approximately 𝑂(𝑛), significantly lower than 𝑂(𝑛2 ).

5.1.2. Baseline methods
   According to the modeling approach of knowledge concept structures, we compare our method with three types of KT models:
pointwise knowledge tracing, implicit knowledge tracing, and explicit knowledge tracing.
   Pointwise Knowledge Tracing (POI-KT): A class of KT models based on the hidden markov model, where each knowledge
concept is treated as an independent variable:

      • BKT: Based on Bayesian networks, BKT (Corbett & Anderson, 1994) models learners’ knowledge states as binary variables for
        each knowledge concept. It is one of the earliest and most fundamental methods in the field of knowledge tracing.

   Implicit Knowledge Tracing (IMP-KT): A class of KT models that implicitly considers the underlying relationships between
knowledge concepts through the hidden layers of the model:

      • DKT: Based on recurrent neural networks (RNN), DKT (Piech et al., 2015) employs deep learning to track learners’ dynamically
        changing knowledge states. It is a pioneering work that introduced deep learning into knowledge tracing.
      • DKVMN: Based on dynamic memory mechanisms, DKVMN (Zhang, Shi, King, & Yeung, 2017) utilizes a dynamic key–value
        memory network to record the interaction history between learners and questions, predicting learners’ performance on new
        questions.
      • DKT+: Based on the forgetting curve, DKT+ (Yeung & Yeung, 2018) incorporates forgetting information to better capture
        learners’ knowledge forgetting patterns, thereby improving prediction accuracy.
      • SAKT: Based on the self-attention mechanism, SAKT (Xu, Ruan, Korpeoglu, Kumar, & Achan, 2019) focuses on the relations
        between learners’ past responses and current questions, predicting learners’ performance on new questions.
      • AKT: Based on the attention mechanism, AKT (Ghosh et al., 2020) captures global dependencies and incorporates temporal
        interval information to model long-range dependencies, thereby improving the performance of KT.
      • KQN: Based on the query mechanism, KQN (Lee & Yeung, 2019) treats questions as queries and dynamically generates learners’
        knowledge states, enabling flexible modeling of knowledge states.
      • LPKT: Based on learning process information, LPKT (Shen et al., 2021) uses data such as timestamps, attempt counts, and
        response durations to accurately track learners’ learning states.
      • SimpleKT: Based on problem variation modeling, SimpleKT (Liu, Liu, Chen, Huang, & Luo, 2023) explicitly captures individual
        differences between problems, making it a simple yet useful baseline model.
      • DTransformer: Based on the Diagnostic Transformer, DTransformer (Yin et al., 2023) builds a hierarchical framework from the
        question level to the knowledge level, explicitly diagnosing learners’ knowledge proficiency for each question and incorporates
        contrastive learning to enhance performance.
      • StableKT: Based on a linear-biased attention mechanism, StableKT (Li, Bai, Guo, Liu et al., 2024) addresses the sequence
        length generalization problem through distance-proportional attention penalties, effectively maintaining stable predictions
        across interaction sequences of varying lengths while enhancing performance.
      • ReKT: Based on human teachers’ decision-making processes and multi-level cognitive development theory, ReKT (Shen et al.,
        2024) comprehensively captures students’ dynamic knowledge states through multi-perspective knowledge state modeling and
        enhances KT performance by designing a lightweight FRU framework to simulate cognitive patterns.

5.1.3. Implementation details
    The model is implemented using the PyTorch framework, and the Adam optimizer (Kingma & Ba, 2014) is employed for
optimization. During the training process, hyperparameter tuning is conducted to determine the optimal configuration. In the
experiments, the embedding dimension 𝑑 is selected from {32, 64, 128, 256}, the learning rate is chosen from {0.0005, 0.001,
0.002}, the weight decay coefficient is selected from {1e–2, 1e–3, 1e–4}, the dropout rate is chosen from {0.1, 0.2, 0.3}, the number
of encoding layers is selected from {1, 2, 3, 4}, the temperature coefficient 𝜏 is chosen from {0.1, 0.5, 1, 2}, and the scaling factor
𝛾 is selected from {1, 2, 4, 8}. For each dataset, it is split into training and testing sets in the ratio of 8:2. Ultimately, the learning
rate is set to 0.001, weight decay is set to 1e–4, the dropout rate is set to 0.1, and the embedding dimension is set to 128. The
number of encoding layers, temperature coefficient 𝜏, and scaling factor 𝛾 are determined based on the specific characteristics of the
dataset, and a more detailed discussion of these aspects is provided in Section 5.4. The sequence length for all learners is uniformly
processed to 100; for students with sequence lengths less than 100, padding with zeros is applied, while sequences longer than 100
are sliced. All experiments are conducted on a PC server equipped with an Intel® Core™ i7-14700K CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce
RTX 4090D GPU. The models are implemented using the PyTorch 2.0.0 framework.
    As predicting learner responses constitutes a binary classification task, we evaluate the effectiveness of HKT using three widely
applied evaluation metrics: area under the ROC curve (AUC), accuracy (ACC), and root mean square error (RMSE). AUC measures
the model’s ability to distinguish between the samples correctly, ACC assesses the overall prediction accuracy, and RMSE evaluates
the variance of predicted values from actual values. Higher AUC and ACC values indicate better performance, while lower RMSE
values are superior. For more details regarding the above metrics, please refer to Cui, Yu et al. (2024).

5.3. Ablation study (Q2)

    We evaluated the contribution of each component to the overall model performance by conducting the following ablation studies:

     • WO-KHT: Removes the Knowledge Hierarchy Tree, generating knowledge concept embeddings without modeling vertical
       hierarchical relations.
     • WO-KAG: Removes the Knowledge Association Graph, generating knowledge concept embeddings without modeling intra-
       layer associations.
     • WO-HCL: Removes the Hierarchical Contrastive Learning Module, generating knowledge concept embeddings without em-
       ploying hierarchical contrastive learning.

    As shown in Table 4, we find:

     1. Removing the KHT reduced the AUC by 0.35%, 0.61%, and 0.56% across the three datasets, highlighting its positive impact
        on capturing vertical knowledge relations, especially in the more complex Eedi and Junyi datasets.
     2. Removing the KAG caused the largest AUC drop of 2.12% in the ASSIST2017 dataset, showing that intra-layer relations are
        more crucial in datasets with simpler hierarchical structures.
     3. Removing the HCL led to performance drops in all datasets, confirming its effectiveness in improving overall model
        performance. The performance drop on the Junyi dataset is smaller compared to the other two datasets. It is due to the
        dataset which has fewer knowledge concepts, especially a scarcity of knowledge concepts at the lower leaf nodes. As a result,
        the sample size for hierarchical contrastive learning is relatively small, leading to a more limited performance decline after
        removing the HCL module.

   Differences in knowledge hierarchies likely drive the varying impact of different components. The ASSIST2017 dataset has fewer
hierarchical levels. Removing the KHT, which models relations across levels, causes only a minor performance drop. In contrast,
the Eedi dataset features more complex hierarchical structures. In this case, removing KHT results in a greater performance drop
compared to component B. These findings indicate that richer knowledge hierarchies enhance the effectiveness of KHT. Meanwhile,
contrastive learning proves to be more effective in datasets with simpler hierarchical structures.

5.7. Model complexity and deployment feasibility analysis


    We performed a unified parameter-based evaluation of the training efficiency and resource consumption of various models on
the ASSIST2017 dataset, with the results shown in Table 5. Overall, our method demonstrates a balanced performance in terms
of training time, inference speed, and computational resource consumption, particularly excelling in single-step inference speed,
where it outperforms most deep learning-based KT methods. It is important to note that the GKT model involves extensive batch
matrix operations and graph convolutional layers, processed step-by-step with only one time step of data being handled at a time.
This results in lower efficiency compared to other models. However, excluding the GKT model, our method still shows excellent
computational efficiency when compared to several baseline models.
    From the perspective of deployment feasibility, our method can be flexibly deployed on current mainstream ITS platforms.
Although our method consists of multiple modules, each one is not complex and follows a clear separation between the front-end
and back-end. Specifically, our approach is composed of two main stages: Graph Embedding and Weight Allocation Networks.
These two components are designed to achieve high cohesion and low coupling. The graph embedding stage only needs to learn the
features of knowledge concepts and provide them to the weight allocation network, which drives the entire model’s operation.
In actual deployment, switching to different graph embedding schemes or subsequent backbone networks will not affect the
overall functionality of the model, as long as the interfaces between them remain consistent. Moreover, our knowledge concept
representation method, based on the KHT and KAG structures, employs an incremental learning approach, allowing for offline
learning with new data and dynamic online deployment. This means our method can be easily adapted to mainstream ITS platforms
and quickly put into operation.

5.8. Case study

    A clearer understanding of the multi-level prediction process is provided by analyzing the response data of two learners from
the Eddi dataset. They have similar response sequences but different outcomes. A detailed comparison of predictions across various
hierarchical knowledge levels is presented in Fig. 6. Comparing the predictions to the actual responses, our weight allocation model
reliably determines the level that best matches each learner’s performance. For example, when answering question 2449, learner
22 887 is mainly influenced by the L4 level, while learner 19 relies more on the L3 level. Interestingly, learner 22 887 answered the
previous question incorrectly, whereas learner 19 answered it correctly. Despite the prior response differences, the model accurately
pinpoints the most relevant level and assigns it the highest weight. It shows that the model’s ability to effectively evaluate the
influence of different levels, even in complex scenarios.
    Meanwhile, the weight allocation provides educators with interpretable instructional assistance information. When learners
answer questions, the weight allocation networks can offer reference weights for different knowledge levels involved in each
question. Through this information, teachers can assess learners’ mastery of various knowledge levels. If a learner answers correctly,
the teacher can analyze their knowledge mastery based on the weight allocation; if the answer is incorrect, the teacher can identify
which knowledge level the learner struggles with and infer the potential cause of the error. For example, when learner 22 887
answered question 2448, the weight allocation indicated that the L4 level knowledge concept might be the area of insufficient
mastery, leading to the incorrect response. Thus, weight allocation helps teachers precisely locate learners’ weaknesses, enabling
targeted guidance and remediation.
    Additionally, analyzing the prediction results at different levels, it was observed that the L1 level exhibits relatively stable
variations compared to other levels. We speculate that it is related to the hierarchical structure in the Eedi. The L1 level contains
only 7 knowledge concepts, which limits the range of embedding options available at this level. As a result, the limited embedding
options at this level likely lead to smoother in prediction outcomes during single-level predictions.

6. Conclusion and future work

   We present the HKT, a hierarchical and interpretable knowledge tracing model that effectively captures both vertical and
horizontal dependencies between knowledge concepts through the integration of a KHT and a KAG. In addition, we have designed
a weight allocation networks that dynamically and accurately assign weights to knowledge at various hierarchical levels, enabling a
comprehensive prediction of learner performance. The experimental results demonstrate that HKT consistently outperforms existing
pointwise, implicit, and explicit knowledge tracing models across multiple publicly available datasets. Further ablation studies
and hyperparameter analysis confirm the importance and robustness of each component within the model. Meanwhile, HKT also
demonstrates good interpretability by explicitly modeling knowledge concepts through KHT and KAG, offering an interpretable
and structured knowledge framework that aligns with human cognition. It then employs a dynamic weight allocation mechanism
to provide educators with a clear and interpretable basis for precisely identifying the learners’ weak knowledge levels. This
work introduces a novel approach to accurately model and predict learners’ knowledge states, offering valuable insights for the
development of personalized education systems.
   In the future, we aim to explore the potential of self-supervised learning to discover knowledge structures. On the one hand,
manually annotated knowledge structures require significant human resources and are inefficient. On the other hand, manually
annotated knowledge structures may not necessarily align well with deep learning models. We look forward to investigating whether
there are differences between the knowledge structures learned by deep learning models and those annotated by humans.
