6. Conclusion
In this study, guided by the principles of knowledge transfer the-
ory, we explored the evolution of knowledge states to predict future
performance. However, traditional graph-based KT methods overlook
the intricate multi-association relationships among entities, leading
to deviations in the overall semantic characteristics of the problem
representation and inappropriate knowledge transfer propagation. This
further results in discrepancies between the actual and represented
knowledge states during the transfer process, ultimately affecting the
prediction performance of the model. To overcome these limitations,
we proposed a novel method called MAHKT, which was designed
to enhance both interpretability and performance. MAHKT effectively
captures the complex and diverse associations between questions and
concepts through a multi-association heterogeneous graph, encompass-
ing prerequisites, similarities, and correlations. The model employs
an attention mechanism to quantify node similarity and leverages
both near and far transfer mechanisms within knowledge propagation
to extract rich associative information. Additionally, MAHKT utilizes
dual LSTM models to independently track the evolution of knowledge
states for both questions and concepts, providing a more accurate
and interpretable representation of learners’ evolving knowledge states.
Extensive experiments conducted on real-world datasets demonstrate
that MAHKT consistently outperforms existing models. Furthermore,
ablation studies have confirmed the significant impact of each module
on the overall model performance.
Even though our MAHKT model has demonstrated strong perfor-
mance, several promising directions for future research remain. First,
learner performance is not solely determined by correct or incorrect
responses; behavioral factors during the learning process also play
a crucial role. Future work could explore the impact of multimodal
learning data, such as clickstream data [63] and response times [28], on
learners’ knowledge states to enable a more comprehensive modeling
of the learning process. Additionally, learners’ responses are often influ-
enced by outlier behaviors, such as guessing or careless mistakes [64].
Investigating these anomalies could enhance the tracking of knowledge
state evolution. Finally, exploring causal inference to understand the
underlying reasons for learners’ performance could help uncover causal
relationships [65] that drive changes in knowledge states, thereby
enabling more interpretable knowledge tracing. These directions hold
significant potential for advancing the field in the future.
