With the rise of AI in education, knowledge tracing (KT) has become important for modeling students’
knowledge from interaction data. However, existing methods still face three major challenges, including limited
modeling of personalized exercise–concept relations, low robustness to noisy interactions, and inefficient
training due to suboptimal data selection. To address these issues, we propose R2GCurL, a novel KT framework
with two key designs. First, we recast KT as a graph classification problem and construct dynamic graphs
from student responses, enabling the model to capture structural relations between exercises and concepts for
more personalized KT. Second, we introduce a data-centric curriculum learning strategy based on dynamic
graph entropy. Under our definition, pairwise dynamic graph entropy measures graph-transition continuity,
where larger values indicate stronger structural similarity. Its sequence-level aggregation is used to derive a
structure-aware difficulty signal for sample scheduling. On top of this, an RL-based scheduler further adapts
batch selection based on model feedback and is especially beneficial under noisier and more unstable training
regimes. Theoretical analysis shows that R2GCurL has lower computational complexity than existing graph-
based KT models. Extensive experiments on five real-world datasets confirm its effectiveness, robustness, and
generalizability, including as a plug-and-play enhancement for sequence-based KT models.

Introduction
Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) support personalized practice, feedback, and recommenda-
tion, and they now underpin many online learning platforms [3, 46, 48]. Within ITS, Knowledge
Tracing (KT) estimates a learner’s evolving knowledge state from historical interactions and
supports downstream tasks such as exercise recommendation, performance prediction, and per-
sonalized intervention [18, 23, 24, 27, 40, 58, 62]. As shown in Figure 1, each column represents an
exercise at a particular timestamp, the colored circles in the concept rows indicate the concepts
associated with that exercise, and the bottom row records whether the student answered correctly
[9, 12, 48].
Traditional KT models, including Bayesian KT [36] and Item Response Theory-based methods
[7, 29], are rooted in cognitive and educational theory, but they often make simplified assumptions
about the learner state [46, 48]. As large-scale educational logs have become available, deep KT
methods have emerged as the dominant way to model students’ evolving knowledge states from
response sequences. Broadly, these methods fall into two groups: sequence-based and graph-based
approaches. Sequence-based models use recurrent networks or self-attention to capture temporal
dependencies [11, 35, 41, 43, 47, 52, 57, 61], but they usually encode exercise–concept structure
only implicitly. Graph-based models instead represent structural dependencies among exercises
and concepts explicitly [10, 46, 48], yet many rely on global or static structures that are not well
suited to student-specific structural evolution over time. This gap motivates our focus on a method
that preserves both temporal order and personalized exercise–concept structure. However, three
data-related challenges remain central in current KT research.
—Data structure limitations in modeling personalized concept relationships. Most sequence-based
KT models focus solely on modeling at the exercise or concept level, without explicitly
capturing the relationships between exercises and concepts. In contrast, graph-based KT
models are capable of representing such complex relationships, but they typically disregard
student-specific interaction sequences and treat all learners as homogeneous. As a result,
they fail to capture the dynamic interplay between (1) the structural dependencies among
knowledge concepts and (2) individual learners’ evolving response histories. This limitation
undermines the models’ ability to infer personalized knowledge states, ultimately reducing
prediction accuracy in real-world educational scenarios.
—Low robustness to noisy data. Real-world online learning systems often encounter data quality
issues, particularly inconsistent or misleading student interactions. Noisy interactions arise
from various sources, including non-cognitive factors (e.g., disengagement) or suboptimal ITS
recommendations that misalign exercise difficulty with a student’s proficiency, either being
too simple to promote the student’s learning or too complex to be effective [4, 17, 30, 66]. These
