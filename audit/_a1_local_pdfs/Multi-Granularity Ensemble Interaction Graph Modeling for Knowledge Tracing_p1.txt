A B S T R A C T
Knowledge tracing (KT) is a crucial educational tool that models students’ mastery of various knowledge
concepts by analyzing their historical learning records. Mainstream studies have turned to Deep Neural
Networks (DNNs) to effectively trace students’ knowledge states. Nonetheless, these methods may encounter
performance barriers due to two critical constraints: the treatment of learning records as singular time series
data and the absence of accounting for interaction correlations. To overcome the limitations, we introduce
a novel KT model, named Multi-Granularity Ensemble Interaction Graph Modeling for Knowledge Tracing
(MGIGKT). Our model incorporates two modules designed to model students’ knowledge states from the
perspectives of temporal perception and interaction dependency, respectively. This approach allows for a more
comprehensive understanding of students’ learning dynamics and the correlations between their interactions.
We have conducted extensive experiments on four datasets to evaluate the superiority and effectiveness of our
proposed MGIGKT model. The results of these experiments provide empirical evidence of the model’s enhanced
ability to trace students’ knowledge states accurately.
1. Introduction
In recent years, the demand for intelligent educational services
among students has surged [1–3]. Consequently, online education sys-
tems have become increasingly vital in addressing this demand. One
such critical component that has come to the forefront is KT, which
provides students with immediate and precise feedback. KT entails
examining students’ historical learning records to evaluate their com-
petency in different knowledge areas, with the goal of forecasting their
academic performance ahead. This not only allows for the delivery of
varied learning resources but also permits the tailoring of individual-
ized learning trajectories to suit personal educational needs. Moreover,
KT underpins adaptive education, streamlining the personalization of
educational content and its complexity in accordance with students’
learning advancements and knowledge levels. This encompasses fea-
tures such as automated test creation and post-lesson tasks, all geared
towards a more focused and impactful learning journey. By harnessing
the power of KT through the analysis of students’ historical learning
records, online education systems can acquire in-depth insights into
their educational states, significantly enhancing the online learning
experience for both students and educators.

Currently, numerous studies have been dedicated to exploring the
task of KT [4–7]. Due to the powerful feature extraction capabilities
of DNNs for modeling complex cognitive processes, recent researches
have predominantly relied on more complex and accurate DNNs ar-
chitectures. These methods can be broadly categorized into two types:
conventional KT methods and graph-based KT methods. The first cat-
egory of conventional KT methods models students’ knowledge states
from a temporal perspective using deep learning techniques, aiming to
dynamically capture the evolving proficiency of students. For example,
DKT [8] is the first work to apply Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)
to the KT task. Due to its simple structure and efficient performance,
it has been widely adopted as a paradigm by subsequent deep learning
KT models. Following this, more types of neural network technologies
have been introduced to improve temporal KT models. For example,
DKVMN [9] introduces a memory-augmented network that uses mem-
ory networks to store and update knowledge concepts and students’
knowledge states. Meanwhile, the SAKT [10] model incorporates an
attention mechanism, which calculates the correlation between the
exercise to be predicted and the historical interactions in the student’s

Despite the significant success of existing KT methods, two major
limitations still need to be addressed: (1) Single granularity modeling of
interaction time information: Current KT models often model the dynamic
changes in students’ knowledge states at only a single granularity.
However, in reality, students’ learning records usually exhibit different
distribution patterns at various granularities. As shown in Fig. 1, we
present the distribution histograms of the learning records of two
students. It can be observed that different students often have different
learning patterns. For example, Bob prefers to study on weekday morn-
ings, while Tom prefers to study extensively on weekend afternoons.
The complexity of these learning patterns poses a challenge to single-
granularity modeling of existing methods. Therefore, fully exploring the
different learning patterns exhibited by students at multiple granular-
ities is crucial for the success of KT models. (2) Insufficient capture of
latent correlations between interactions: To achieve good predictions for
the next exercise, studies have shown that considering the correlations
between historical interactions is highly beneficial [11–13]. Existing
models fail to fully uncover the deep connections between historical in-
teractions, such as knowledge concept relationships between exercises.
More specifically, these inherent correlations include shared knowledge
concepts between exercises and the sequential relationships between
the contained knowledge concepts. Meanwhile, students’ interaction
sequence not only include the exercises but also their responses to
these exercises. Therefore, when evaluating the correlation between
interactions, it is essential to consider both the knowledge concept
relationships and the correctness of the responses. To illustrate the im-
