Knowledge Tracing In recent years, deep neural network (DNN) has found more and more applications in diverse ﬁelds, including knowledge tracing (KT). KT aims to capture a student’s knowledge state according to his interactive questions and predict his response to a new question. DKT [7], the ﬁrst attempt to employ RNN-based methods in KT, utilizes the LSTM network to model the interactive questions sequences. DKVMN [9] leverages the memory-augmented network to retain long-term dependencies in historical records. Additionally, the attention-based methods, like SAKT [11] and AKT [12], introduce attention mechanisms to highlight these historical interactions that are highly relevant to the target question. ECD [22] proposes an educational context-aware cognitive diagnosis framework to learn the cognitive states from the concerned educational context and stu- dents’ historical learning records simultaneously. ABKT [23] introduces the ability factor into the learning feedback attribu- tion and analyze the learning process from knowledge and ability. HawkesKT [24] investigates ﬁne-grained temporal cross- effects between different skills in KT and explicitly models these cross-effects by the point process. SEEP [25] fuses questions and concepts to obtain semantic-enhanced question embeddings. GIKT [13] builds a r

HyperRec [31] utilizes a sequential hypergraph to generate the dynamic item embeddings for next-item recommendation and DHCN [32] combines hypergraph with a line graph to capture the intra- and inter- information of sessions for session-based recommendation. Contrastive Learning As an emerging paradigm of self-supervised learning, contrastive learning aims to learn discriminative representations by contrasting positive and negative pairs. It was ﬁrstly used in visual representation learning, in which negative samples are generated by cropping, resizing, rotation, etc. Later, contrastive framework is extended to enhance graph representation learning via self-discrimination. DGI [20] is the ﬁrst to introduce the InfoMax principle into GNN and augment the original graph by simply shufﬂing node features. GMI [33] measures the mutual information between a center node and its local patch from node-level and topology-level features. MVGRL [34] applies a graph diffusion network (GDN) to generate addi- tional structural views. GCA [35] optimizes the uniform data augmentation schemes with the adaptive mechanism. HeCo [36] considers two views of an HIN, including network schema and meta-path views, so as to capture local and high-level structures simultaneously. CL4KT [37] introduces contrastive learning into KT and conducts the data augmentation on stu- dents’ learning history sequence to reveal semantical similarity. But CL4KT cannot fuse the graph structure information and capture the latent relations between nodes. 3. Y. Han et al. 2. Related work 2.1. Knowledge tracing Machine Learning-Based Knowledge Tracing. As a pioneering work of knowledge tracing, Albert et al. proposed BKT [4], which used Bayesian networks and hidden Markov models to estimate a student’s cognition level on a specific skill. Similarly, Michael et al. [19] developed an individualized Bayesian knowledge tracing model by incorporating more student features into BKT to boost the prediction performance on unseen students. Afterward, some variants based on BKT have emerged, such as introducing students’ learning ability [20], the effect of exercise difficulty [21], and prior knowledge and relations among skills [22]. Meanwhile, the logistic model [23] is developed for knowledge tracing, which utilizes logistic functions to measure the probability that a student correctly answering exercises. Jill et al. proposed KTM [24], an extensible knowledge tracing model based on factorization machines, to quickly trace students’ knowledge evolution patterns. Although these initial approaches create the breakthrough in knowledge tracing, they over-rely on narrow linear models with limited capability in learning students’ cognition evolution [5], which inevitably results in inefficient performance of knowledge tracing. Deep Learning-Based Knowledge Tracing. With the significant surge in deep learning, various types of neural networks have been cultivated to model students’ cognition patterns for knowledge trac- ing [3]. Jill et al. proposed DKT [5], the path-breaking knowledge tracing framework using recurrent neural networks (RNNs). DKT as- sumed that students have the same learning ability and utilized hidden neural units to capture students’ cognitive state over time and predicted their capability to master specific skills. To relax their assumption, Jill et al. further presented Deep-DKT [25], a deeper DKT incorporated dynamic student classification based on their different learning abili- ties. Different from DKT and Deep-DKT, dynamic key–value memory networks (DKVMN [26]) and sequential key–value memory networks (STVMN [6]) were proposed to incorporate the strengths from recurrent neural networks and memory networks for capturing dynamic knowl- edge evolution patterns. As attention mechanism [27] has achieved remarkable success in deep learning, it has been introduced into the knowledge tracing field. Particularly, Ghosh et al. proposed a context- aware attention knowledge tracing method (AKT [7]), using monotonic attention to obtain better prediction results and improve interpretabil- ity. Afterward, Pandey et al. proposed SAKT [8], a knowledge trac- ing framework based on a self-attention mechanism, which utilized self-attention to learn knowledge concepts from students’ historical interactions to address data sparsity in knowledge tracing. Further- more, several studies incorporated deep sequence models to boost the performance of knowledge tracing, including the text content of exer- cises [28,29], temporal dependence between skills [30], and students’ individual learning ability and knowledge acquisition behaviors [31]. These methods can effectively obtain isolated deep features, but the interdependent relationships among knowledge objects ( e.g., students, exercises, skills) are ignored, making their performance limited. Knowledge tracing Knowledge Tracing is a fundamental task in intelligent tutoring systems, aiming to dynamically infer a learner’s knowledge state based on their learning interactions (Jiang, Wei, Zhang, & Zhang, 2024; Wang & Sahebi, 2023; Zhao, Xia, Jiang, & He, 2023). With the rapid accumulation of educational data and advancements in deep learning techniques, KT methods have evolved from traditional statistical models to sophisticated deep learning-based approaches (Wang et al., 2023). Consequently, their predictive accuracy tends to be suboptimal in scenarios involving more sophisticated knowledge interdependencies. In general, prior studies on knowledge tracing can be grouped into three categories: traditional non-deep learning methods, deep sequential modeling methods, and deep graph-based methods. 2.1.1 Traditional Methods. As the pioneering work for knowledge tracing, BKT [7] was proposed based on the hidden Markov model that used binary variables to represent students’ knowledge states, i.e., whether a knowledge concept is mastered or not. BKT considered four factors affecting students’ responses: the initial knowledge level, learning rate, guessing proba- bility, and slipping probability. After that, many researchers extended BKT by introducing other important factors, such as students’ individual variation in ability [17], the difficulty level of exercises [34], and prerequisite hierarchies and relationships within knowledge concepts [16]. Anothertypicalkindoftraditionalmethodsislogisticmodels[ 32],whichdirectlyadoptedlogistic functions to predict the probability of correctly answering exercises according to the side infor- mation about students and knowledge concepts. Furthermore,knowledge tracing machines (KTM) [40] was developed to take advantage of factorization machines [3] to generalize logistic models to characterize the pairwise feature interactions for KT. Despite the initial breakthrough of traditional methods, they mostly relied on linear models with restricted functional forms [33], which inevitably hindered the advancement of knowledge tracing. 2.1.2 Sequential Modeling Methods.
