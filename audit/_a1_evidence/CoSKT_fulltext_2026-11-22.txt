CoSKT: A Collaborative Self-Supervised
Learning Method for Knowledge Tracing
Chunyun Zhang ,H e b oM a , Chaoran Cui ,Y u m oY a o ,W e i r a nX u , Y unfeng Zhang , and Y uling Ma
Abstract—Knowledge tracing (KT) aims to trace students’ evolv-
ing knowledge states based on their learning sequences. Recently,
some deep learning based models have been proposed to incor-
porate the historical information of individuals to trace students’
knowledge states and achieve encouraging progress. However, these
works ignore the collaborative information among those students
who have similar exercise–answering experiences, which may con-
tain some valuable information. In this article, we present a novel
collaborative self-supervised learning method for KT (CoSKT),
which exploits both similar students’ collaborative information and
individual information to improve KT. We ﬁrst use the overlap rate
of students’ learning experiences to retrieve similar students. Based
on similar students’ exercise–answering sequences, we leverage
attention mechanism to learn the representation of their common
knowledge state and expected response to the target exercise.
Then, we introduce self-supervised learning by encouraging the
consistency between the common knowledge state and individual
knowledge state. Finally, we integrate collaborative information
and individual knowledge state with a gate mechanism to con-
duct the response prediction of the target exercise. We compare
CoSKT with nine existing KT methods on three public datasets,
and the results show that CoSKT achieves the state-of-the-art
performance.
Index Terms—Autoregressive model, collaborative information,
knowledge tracing (KT), self-attention, self-supervised learning
(SSL).

KT has been researched for several decades. Early KT meth-
ods [2], [3] are based on shallow machine learning methods,
including probabilistic method and logistic method. The most
representative probabilistic method is Bayesian KT (BKT) [2].
It considers the learning process as a Markov process and

the dynamics of students’ knowledge states. Logistic methods

consideration, thereby facilitating the estimation of the mastery
level of a particular knowledge concept [4]. With the wide
application of deep learning, many deep learning methods [5],
[6], [7], [8] are utilized to improve KT. Deep KT (DKT) is the
ﬁrst proposed DKT model, which introduces recurrent neural
networks (RNNs) to model student learning [5]. After that, a
diverse array of neural networks is developed to improve KT.
In general, current deep learning-based KT methods are divided
into two classes: sequential modeling method and graph-based
method. Sequential modeling methods use the hidden state
of deep sequence framework, such as RNNs [5] or memory-
augmented neural networks [9], to represent knowledge state.
Some of them further introduce attention mechanism to consider
the relevant exercises in past interactions [7], [10]. Graph-based
methods leverage graph structure [11], [12] to incorporate the
relationships of exercises and concepts as a prior information to
improve KT. Overall, these methods achieve promising results
and greatly improve the performance of KT.
Despite the encouraging progress, the performance of current
KT methods based on deep learning is far from satisfactory.
One of the major limitations in existing research lies in that
they generally consider the individual information learned from
students’ own exercise–answering history, and ignore the fact
that students with similar exercise–answering experiences have
comparable grasp of concepts and acquire similar knowledge
states. We named this kind of information as collaborative
information, which can offer some valuable clues in tracing
students’ knowledge state. Intuitively, an experienced teacher
does not assess students’ mastery of knowledge only based
on their individual performances; instead, he or she usually

1) We propose a novel collaborative self-supervised KT

information captured from similar students together with
students’ individual information to improve the prediction
accuracy (ACC) of next exercise responses.
2) We leverage SSL to encourage the consistency between the
common knowledge state and individual knowledge state,
which provides extra supervision for better representation
learning.
3) Extensive experimental results on three public datasets
show that CoSKT can achieve a new state-of-the-art per-
formance compared with nine existing methods.

II. R ELA TEDWORK
Since our proposed KT methods are based on SSL, we
ﬁrst thoroughly review KT literature, and then brieﬂy give an
overview of SSL.
A. Knowledge Tracing
Existing KT methods are generally divided into two groups:
shallow learning-based KT methods and deep learning-based
KT methods.
Shallow learning-based method: Shallow learning-based KT
methods are mainly based on shallow machine learning methods
by explicitly considering different kinds of learning factors. One
of the groups is a probabilistic method. The most representative
probabilistic method is BKT [2]. As the pioneering work of
KT, BKT can be seen as an application of HMM. With con-
sidering four important factors affecting students’ responses,
it modeled students mastering a knowledge concept or not as a
two-state framework. Hereafter, many following BKT variations
were developed by integrating other important factors, such as
student individualization [13], prior difﬁculty of exercises [14],
and intrinsic relationship between knowledge concepts [9].A n -
other typical kind of shallow learning-based method is logistic
model [4], [15], which utilized a logistic function to encode
different factors in students’ exercise–answering sequences.
Furthermore, KT machines (KTM)[16] were leveraged to gener-
alize logistic models to a higher dimension by using factorization
machines [17] to make use of other available side information.
Nevertheless, even though shallow learning-based methods have
achieved preliminary progress, most shallow-based methods are

Deep learning-based method: Deep learning is suited to
modeling the complex learning process beneﬁting from its
powerful ability in feature representation [13], [18]. Recently,
many DKT models have been proposed and reach outstanding
performance [5], [6], [19], [20]. These methods are generally
based on the autoregressive architecture, and can be classed into
two categories: sequential modeling method and graph-based
method.
1) Sequential Modeling Method: As the representative se-
quential modeling method, DKT [5] was the ﬁrst study,
which introduced deep framework into KT. It considered
the hidden state of long short-term memory (LSTM) [21]
unit at each time step as students’ changing knowledge
state and yielded signiﬁcantly enhanced results in com-
parison with traditional methods. To enhance the inter-
pretability of DKT, Zhang et al. [9] utilized a dynamic
key-value memory network (DKVMN), which can output
students’ mastery of every concept with exploiting the
correlations of these concepts. With the great popularity
of attention mechanism [22], many KT methods utilize
attention to enhance the performance of KT [7], [10].
One of the typical methods is the self-attentive KT model
(SAKT) [10], which leveraged self-attention [22] mech-
anism to predict a student’s next exercise response by
using all relational exercises from his or her historical
interactions. Unlike SAKT using the scaled inner-product
attention strategy of the classical transformer framework,
attentive knowledge tracing model (AKT) [7] introduced
exponential decay into transformer network to model the
attenuation of a student’s knowledge states over time.
Furthermore, to improve the performance of classical se-
quential deep learning-based KT models, various studies
try to take more elaborate factors related to learning into
consideration, such as the text description of exercises[6],
prerequisite relations between concepts [23], and other
side information collected during learning interaction [9].
2) Graph-Based Method: Since knowledge concepts are
correlated to each other, there exists a graph structure
among all the knowledge concepts (KCs). Hence, several
graph-based methods are proposed to model the latent
relationship of knowledge concepts with a graph struc-
ture to improve the performance of KT [11], [19].T h e
graph-based KT (GKT) method cast the latent knowledge
structure as a graph and viewed KT as an application of the
graph neural network [11]. Similarly, Wang et al.[19] pre-
sented deep hierarchical KT model (DHKT) based on the
classical DKT, which learned representations of exercises
and concepts separately with modeling the relationships
of exercises and concepts as a graph. After that, following
the similar architecture with DHKT, pre-training embed-
dings via bipartite graph model (PEBG) [12] formulated
the exercise–concept relationships as a bipartite graph
and introduced a product-based network to pretrain ex-
ercise embeddings. Besides, motivated by the transfer and
knowledge theory [18] in education ﬁeld, Tong et al. [24]
utilized structure-based knowledge tracing model (SKT)
to model the affect propagation between concepts by cap-
turing the multiple relationships in knowledge structure.
However, current deep learning-based methods mainly con-
sider students’ dynamic individual knowledge state by mod-
eling their own exercise–answering history. They ignore the
collaborative information among students who have analogous
exercise–answering experiences, which can provide some ad-
ditional information in predicting students’ next exercise re-
sponses. In this article, different from existing deep learning
KT methods, we proposed a collaborative KT model, which
explicitly utilizes students’ collaborative information including
common knowledge state and expected response to improve KT
by modeling learning sequences of similar students.
B. Self-Supervised Learning
SSL becomes a popular learning strategy due to its pow-
erful capacity of representation learning in recent years [25].
It leverages a large amount of unlabeled data itself as super-
vised information and beneﬁts various downstream tasks [26],
[27], [28]. Liu et al. [29] divided the mainstream SSL meth-
ods to three groups: generative method, contrastive method,
and generative–contrastive (adversarial) method. Recent break-
throughs in methods [30], [31], [32], [33], such as momen-
tum contrastive Learning method (MoCo), simple framework
for contrastive learning method (SimCLR), and swapping as-
signments between multiple views of the same image method
(SwA V), show the potential of contrastive learning models for
representation. In general, contrastive learning tries to learn a
good representation, which is able to recognize the same sample
while distinguishing itself from other samples [34]. As a result,
most contrastive learning methods focus on designing agent
tasks to construct positive pairs or design objective functions
to make positive samples close in the representation space [29].
SimCLR [30] and MoCo [31] are two of the most representative
works in contrastive learning. Speciﬁcally, SimCLR leveraged
a simple contrastive learning framework to simplify previous
approaches to their essential core and improved upon their
performance. It minimized the distance of positive sample pairs
and maximized the distance of negative sample pairs by using
the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy (CE) [30] loss
as the training loss, which is well known as info noise contrastive
estimation (InfoNCE) [25], [35]. Following the paradigm of
SimCLR, we introduce contrastive learning into KT to make
use of collaborative information in an implicit way. Distinguish
from traditional contrastive learning methods trying to design
positive samples by making data augmentation from original
sample, we consider a student’s individual knowledge state and
the common knowledge state of his or her similar students as
a positive pair. By using the InfoNCE loss as the optimization
objective, the CoSKT model learns a good representation of
knowledge state by pulling similar knowledge states closer and
pushing dissimilar knowledge states farther away.

IV . EXPERIMENTS
In this section, we describe how we set up our experiments,
report a series of experimental results, and present detailed
corresponding analysis.
A. Dataset
In this article, we verify the proposed model on three public
datasets as used in [36] (their detailed statistical information are
illustrated in Table I).
ASSIST091 is a mostly used dataset, which is gathered
from the ASSISTments online tutoring 2 platform during the
2009–2010 school year. Note that if the length of a student’s
exercise–answering sequence is too short, it is difﬁcult for KT
models to accurately model his or her knowledge states. In
prior studies, such as DHKT [12] and PEBG [19], students
with a learning sequence length less than three were excluded
from datasets. To ensure a fair comparison, we followed their
suggested experimental settings.

ASSIST173 is gathered from the competition of ASSIST-

EdNet4 is a large dataset released in [37]. It contains 130
million records generated by 0.78 million students. Due to the

For all datasets, we randomly set 80% as training and valida-

B. Evaluation Metric
The ACC and the area under ROC curve (AUC) [8] are used
as the evaluation metrics in this article. Speciﬁcally, ACC is
the rate of the correct predicted samples. AUC is a measure
of the proportion of true positives (or negatives) that are accu-
rately identiﬁed as such. Given that the prediction of students’
responses is conceptualized as a binary classiﬁcation problem, a
score of 50% is indicative of the performance achievable through
random guessing.
C. Baselines
We choose nine KT methods as baseline methods, includ-
ing two shallow learning-based KT methods and seven deep
learning-based KT method.
1) BKT [2] can be viewed as a variant of HMM. It treats
students’ knowledge states as a variable with two values
and models each knowledge concept separately.
2) KTM [16] tries to make use of the side information with
factorization machine to improve KT performance. In
order to be consistent with KTM, only the identities (IDs)
of exercise and concept are used as side information in
comparison.
3) DKT [5] learns students’ knowledge states through in-
putting their exercise–answering interactions into LSTM
units.
4) DKVMN [9] leverages a memory-augmented framework
to model the update process of students’ grasp of knowl-
edge concepts with two matrices of key and value.
5) SAKT [10] introduces the self-attention mechanism to
place greater emphasis on the relevant history interactions
when makes prediction.
6) AKT [7] introduces a monotonic attention strategy into the
transformer framework by using an exponential decay to
model attenuation of the knowledge states over time.
7) CoKT [8] models interstudent information from learning
sequences of similar students and leverages inter and
intrastudent information to improve KT.
8) GKT [11] leverages graph convolutional neural networks
(GCNs) to cast KT as a time series node-level classi-
ﬁcation problem by learning the relationships between

H. Discussions on Cold Start Problem
The cold start problem is a signiﬁcant issue in KT, particularly
in learning environments with an insufﬁcient number of students
or target students with short learning sequences. In the ﬁrst
scenario, the limited number of students within the learning
environments may hinder KT models from retrieving a sufﬁcient
number of similar students. This situation is analogous to when
we intentionally set a small value for the number of similar
students K in our CoSKT model. In both cases, KT models
cannot make use of sufﬁcient collaborative information. As
depicted in Fig. 5, whenK is small, the performance of CoSKT
deteriorates compared with the optimal scenario, highlighting
the impact of the cold start problem on our method. In the
second case, KT models encounter difﬁculties when attempting
to accurately model a student’s knowledge states if his or her
exercise–answering sequence is too short. Consequently, they
cannot retrieve reliable similar students, resulting in unreliable
collaborative information. This can degrade the performance
of KT models. To illustrate this point effectively, we present
Fig. 6, which depicts the impact of learning sequence length on
the performance of our CoSKT. From Fig. 6, we can see that
the performance of CoSKT is not satisfying when the learning
sequence length is merely 5. However, as the length value in-
creases, the performance exhibits an initial signiﬁcant improve-
ment, followed by a gradual saturation. Notably, we limit our
performance analysis to the learning sequence length less than
50, aligning with the suggested length of 50 in DKT framework.

one possible strategy involves incorporating students’ side in-
formation during modeling their learning sequences. Generally,
the extant public KT datasets encompass students’ performance
data, including exercises and student answers, alongside various

V. C ONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed CoSKT to improve the performance
of KT by making use of collaborative information among stu-
dents with similar exercise–answering history. CoSKT ﬁrst re-
trieves similar students and learns their common knowledge state
and expected response. Then, it introduces SSL to encourage the
consistency between individual knowledge state and the com-
mon knowledge state. The modules of collaborative information
extraction and SSL indeed beneﬁt the performance of KT and our
CoSKT achieves a new state of the art performance. However,
note that our CoSKT only utilizes students’ performance data
and employs the classical DKT framework to model knowledge
states. In our future work, we will explore more elaborate
network architectures and incorporate side information, such as
response time, to more accurately model the evolving knowledge
states of students.
