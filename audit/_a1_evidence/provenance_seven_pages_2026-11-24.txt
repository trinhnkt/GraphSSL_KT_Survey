3.2.3. Interaction graph dependency modeling (IGDM)
As previously mentioned, we believe that in real online education
systems, there are latent correlations between the knowledge concepts
involved in interactions. In addition to knowledge concept informa-
tion, interaction data also includes information on whether students
answered the exercises correctly. If we group interactions solely based
on their association with knowledge concepts, we might overlook the
correctness of the student’s responses, leading to the loss of important
answer outcome information [29]. To more accurately describe the
complex relationships in students’ historical interactions, we introduce
an Interaction Graph Dependency Modeling module that can describe
the relationships between interactions. Specifically, we construct two
types of interaction graphs: one to represent the similarities between
interactions and the other to describe their dissimilarities. Through
these two graphs, we cannot only capture the direct correlations in
students’ historical interactions but also reveal the latent, deeper corre-
lations. We apply Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to these two graphs,
performing low-pass and high-pass filtering operations. Specifically, we
use the dissimilarity matrix to push apart the representations of dis-
similar nodes, reducing interference from dissimilar neighbors, and the
similarity matrix to bring the representations of similar nodes closer.
This approach allows us to obtain distinctive representations of stu-
dents’ knowledge states. We further optimize these representations by
employing a contrastive learning mechanism. This mechanism provides
additional supervisory signals to the model by comparing knowledge
state representations across different interaction graphs, guiding the
model to generate more consistent and meaningful representations.
Consequently, the model can predict students’ future learning per-
formance with greater accuracy, as it can now capture the subtle
differences and complexities in the knowledge states.

3.1. Problem formulation
In an online education platform, each question 𝑞𝑖 corresponds to one 
or more knowledge concepts. Learners acquire the relevant knowledge 
concepts by answering the questions. Let 𝑄 = {𝑞1,𝑞 2, …,𝑞𝑛} denote the 
question set containing 𝑛 questions, where 𝑞𝑖 denotes the 𝑖th question, 
which is a 𝑘-dimensional one-hot vector, i.e., 𝑞𝑖 ∈ R𝑘. The learner’s 
(question, answer) sequence is denoted as 𝑆 = {( 𝑞1,𝑎 1), (𝑞2,𝑎 2), …,
(𝑞𝑡,𝑎𝑡)}, where 𝑞𝑖 ∈ 𝑄 denotes the question answered by the learner 
at timestamp 𝑖, and 𝑡 is the most recent timestamp. Each learner can 
answer the question of interest at a specific time and give a correct or 
incorrect response, denoted as 𝑎𝑖 ∈ {0, 1}. If 𝑎𝑖 = 0, the answer is wrong; 
if 𝑎𝑖 = 1, the answer is correct. Given a learner’s past learning sequence 
𝑆 and a new question 𝑞𝑡+1, we need to model the learner’s historical 
learning sequences to trace the changes in their knowledge state over 
time. Our goal is to predict the probability 𝑃 (𝑎𝑡+1 = 1 ∣ 𝑆,𝑞𝑡+1) that the 
learner will answer the new question correctly based on the current 
knowledge state.

3.3. Forming associated information
DGCNN [57] exploits directional information in directed graphs for 
node representation learning and graph classification tasks. The core 
idea of DGCNN is to aggregate the neighbor information of the nodes 
through a convolutional operation, which considers the direction of 
the edges. This allows DGCNN to distinguish between incoming and 
outgoing edges and thus better capture the topology of the directed 
graph. It uses a convolutional layer to process the neighbor information 
of the nodes to generate a representation of the nodes. Moreover, 
relationships between knowledge concepts are typically directional. For 
instance, ‘‘addition’’ is a prerequisite for ‘‘multiplication’’, but not vice 
versa. GCNs disregard edge directions, potentially leading to confusion 
in information propagation. DGCNN preserves edge direction informa-
tion, allowing knowledge concept representations to aggregate along 
correct paths, thus more accurately depicting knowledge structures.

3.5. Stable cognition
In cognitive processes, learners’ cognitive activities and knowledge 
states are not always in perfect alignment. For instance, learners some-
times overestimate their abilities (high cognitive activity with a low 
knowledge state) or underestimate themselves due to frustrations (low 
cognitive activity with a high knowledge state). Simply merging these 
two states lead to prediction biases. As observed in Fig. 3, various pa-
rameters operate independently, advancing according to specific rules 
within their respective domains, potentially resulting in mismatches 
between learners’ knowledge states and cognitive activities.
Inspired by the literature [17], an Erase-add Gate is designed to 
address this mismatch issue. The core concept of the Erase-add Gate is 
to align learners’ current knowledge states and cognitive activities by 
dynamically adjusting the contributions of different situational factors. 
This mechanism effectively resolves the mismatch problem by allowing 
the model to dynamically ‘‘erase’’ irrelevant information and ‘‘add’’ 
important new information at each time step. Specifically, when a 
learner’s cognitive activity level exceeds their actual knowledge state, 
the Erase-add Gate reduces the weight of cognitive activity while in-
creasing the influence of the knowledge state, thereby avoiding overly 
optimistic predictions. Conversely, when a learner’s knowledge state 
surpasses their cognitive activity performance, the mechanism appro-
priately enhances the contribution of cognitive activity to prevent the 
model from underestimating the learner’s potential.
This dynamic adjustment process simulates selective attention and 
information update mechanisms in human cognition, enabling the 
model to more accurately reflect changes in learners’ cognitive states 
across different learning stages. Specifically, the operation of the Erase-

4.1 Overview of AEGOT-CDKT framework

as the input of the cross-domain prediction model, so as to construct a comprehensive and
complementary representation of the students’ knowledge state to predict their performance
on the exercises.

B. Overview of GraphCA
In this work, we tend to answer the following counterfac-
tual question, “what the student representation would be if we
intervene on the observed answers”. As we know, conducting
counterfactual learning usually has to explore the following
three factors: context, treatment, and outcome, where the con-
text is the given background variable; the treatment is the
independent variable, which is randomized; and the outcome
is the dependent variable, which is related to treatment. In our
method, we treat the student node as the context X, whether
the student’s answering records have been intervened as the
treatment T, and the student’s representation as the outcome Y.
To answer “what the student representation would be”, we
construct the augmentation views on the basis of two factual
observations. Our counterfactual transformation strategies are
developed based on existing causal inference theory, and they
also follow the SUTVA [62] assumption. The overall architec-
ture of our GraphCA is shown in Fig. 3. Specifically, we first
find the questions (from unanswered questions) that are simi-
lar to the ones she has answered. Then, we disturb her histori-
cal answers by the following two strategies: 1) We randomly
replace the questions in her historical answer record with sim-
ilarly unanswered questions to generate the unobserved
answer sequence. As we replace the questions with similar
ones, we deem the disturbed sequence is similar to the origi-
nal one. 2) Another way to obtain unobserved student answer
records is to disrupt the answer sequence of the same ques-
tion set. As the disturbed answer record has the same question
set, we also deem this answer sequence has a similar meaning
to the original one. After that, we achieve students’ counter-
factual representations by performing a graph convolution
layer on the transformed graph, where we treat the counterfac-
tual representations from the above generated answer
sequences as positive samples, and their representation trans-
formation forms as negative samples (the details can be seen
in Section III-E-3). Then, we further conduct contrastive
learning on the original and counterfactual representations to
model students more accurately and robustly.

4.2. Base feature extractor
Formally, given an input graph 𝐵𝐺 = (𝑉,𝐸,𝜆 ) and a GNN model
𝐺𝑁𝑁𝜃, the key idea of majority graph neural networks (GNNs) is to ag-
gregate feature information from a node’s direct (first-order) neighbors,
such as inductive representation learning on large graphs (GraphSAGE)
or Graph Attention network (GAT) (Hamilton, Ying, & Leskovec, 2017;
Veliković et al., 2017). However, they cannot aggregate information
from different nodes and are vulnerable to neighbor nodes (Zhang,
Song, Huang, Swami, & Chawla, 2019). A basic feature extraction
module based on a heterogeneous GNN approach is used for question
feature and skill processing, as shown in Fig. 3. The extractor takes
the item sequence {𝑞1,𝑞 2, …,𝑞𝑖} and skill sequence {𝑐1,𝑐 2, …,𝑐𝑖} as
inputs. Heterogeneous graphical information is used as additional
input, learner nodes and interactions between learners, items and re-
sponses are included. The output of the feature extractor module is
an embedding of items and skills, containing the higher-order features
after being crossed.
The first contribution based on the intuition is that the differentia-
tion of skills and items is not only related to themselves, but also to the
interaction sequence. The original graph is heterogeneous, and more
information about the features related to the current input sequence
can be found on the graph from the input items and skills. So inspired
by Heterogeneous Graph Transformer (HGT), the global sampling was
introduced in base feature extractor (BFE) which based on multi-hop
sampling of small batches of input sequences, to obtain feature data
according to richness (Hu, Dong, Wang, & Sun, 2020). After multi-hop
sampling, the sampled data and the original data are fed separately into
a heterogeneous graph neural network for information aggregation,
where different skills and item embeddings contain features of their
neighboring related nodes. The raw data use masks to prevent the
relationships to be predicted from being captured in advance.

2.3. Graph-based knowledge tracing
Recently, research on knowledge tracing has focused on exploring
the use of graph structures to capture more information.
The model proposed by Chanaa and Faddouli (2020) first constructs
a dynamic graph that changes over time, where each node represents
a student, the nodes are fixed, but the edges are dynamic. Both nodes
and graph topology are transforming over time, matching the knowl-
edge tracing of students. The model learns feature representation by
aggregating each student (i.e., node) and its neighbors, then extracting
the network topology information at each different time step. Finally,
the representation of each node is sent to the classifier to determine
the student’s knowledge state. Nakagawa et al. ( 2019) proposed GKT.
This model sets the course exercises as a graph structure 𝐺 = (𝑉,𝐸 ).
The 𝑛 skills are the nodes 𝑉 = { 𝑣1, …,𝑣𝑛} of the graph, and the
shared dependency of these skills is the edge 𝐸 ⊆ 𝑉 × 𝑉 of the
graph. Assume that the student independently has the knowledge state

between exercises. Furthermore, they all ignore the graph structure
formed during the students’ exercise answering sessions.

4. Session graph-based knowledge tracing model
To address the problem defined in Section 3 , we propose SGKT, a
session graph-based knowledge tracing framework. As shown in Fig. 3,
SGKT comprises three parts: the HRG embedding module (for exercises
and skills modeling), the SG embedding module (for student knowl-
edge state modeling), and the prediction module. The HRG embedding
module is used to learn the embedding of exercise-to-exercise and
skill-to-skill relational information from HRG. It generates the represen-
tation vector, and then employs self-attention mechanisms with FM to
add dynamic semantics to them. The SG embedding module is used to
generate the students’ knowledge state. The prediction module predicts
the probability of students answering the next exercise correctly. The
SG embedding and prediction modules are jointly optimized during
training, where the exercises and skills embeddings are dynamically
obtained from the HRG embedding module.
4.1. HRG embedding module
This module uses the GCN model to generate the embedding repre-
sentation of the nodes in the HRG composed of students, exercises, and
skills. Before embedding HRG, we first define neighbors according to
the meta relationship path in Definition 2, where three meta-relational
paths are defined, namely eSe, eKe, and kEk. Specifically, we process
the dataset according to the following rules: the neighbors of an ex-
ercise are the exercises answered by the same student, i.e., eSe, or
the exercises that require the same skill, i.e., eKe. A skill’s neighbor
is that skill covered by the same exercise, i.e., kEk. Therefore, during
the propagation of the GCN, we use two matrices, one is the exercise–
exercise relation matrix, and the other is the exercise–skill relation
matrix. As shown in the upper part of Fig. 3 , although HRG is a
tripartite graph, the embedding layer of this module only focuses on the
state of exercises and skills, and students are regarded as a connection
between exercises. In the matrices of exercises and skills propagated in
GCN, the neighbors of an exercise are the other exercises answered by
the same student and related skills. The neighbors of skills are all the
exercises related to it. To extract the high-order information, we use
GCN to generate the embedding of exercises and skills i.e.,
