A B S T R A C T
Knowledge tracing presents a fundamental research challenge within personalized education, aiming to
dynamically monitor students’ evolving mastery of individual skills through analysis of their online answer
data. Noteworthy advancements have been achieved in knowledge tracing models, particularly with the
integration of deep learning techniques. These models leverage long-short-term memory (LSTM) networks to
process students’ answer sequences, discern latent skill states, and predict subsequent responses. Nevertheless,
real-world scenarios introduce a substantial challenge, given the inherent sparsity of student interaction data,
implying that each student interacts with only a limited number of skills. Deep knowledge tracing models,
when confronted with sparse data, tend to exhibit a bias towards learning from students with large sample
sizes, consequently resulting in compromised learning outcomes due to insufficient interaction data. Addressing
these challenges, this paper introduces an innovative self-paced learning data augmentation approach designed
to augment the number of student answer sequence samples. Additionally, the paper employs self-supervised
contrastive learning to strike a balance between local and global information, thereby mitigating the model’s
inclination to overly focus on local samples. To effectively address the aforementioned challenges, the paper
proposes a deep knowledge tracing framework grounded in self-paced contrastive learning. The efficacy of
the self-paced contrastive learning strategy is validated using real datasets, demonstrating superior prediction
accuracy compared to alternative algorithms.
1. Introduction
In recent decades, the concept of intelligent education has gar-
nered increasing attention. Traditional one-to-many teaching methods
in classrooms have faced challenges in adapting to the escalating need
for personalized learning experiences among students. The widespread
adoption of online education has resulted in the accumulation of a sub-
stantial repository of continuous student response records. Analyzing
these records to monitor shifts in students’ comprehension of diverse
knowledge concepts has become a foundational task in educational data
mining, prompting the emergence of research in knowledge tracing.
The primary goal of knowledge tracing is to employ advanced big data
mining techniques to extract students’ underlying knowledge structures
from the vast pool of response data [1,2]. Its mathematical representa-

providing a correct response to the next question, based on their his-

been proposed to tackle this challenge. For example, Early Bayesian
Knowledge Tracing (BKT) conceptualized students’ response patterns
as a hidden Markov process, assuming a binary knowledge state that

2. Related work
2.1. Deep knowledge tracing
The Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT) model was originally intro-
duced by Piech et al. [ 5]. As a pioneering deep learning solution
for addressing the knowledge tracing problem. Piech et al. concep-
tualized the knowledge tracing challenge as managing the sequence
of students’ responses to questions. They employed Long Short-Term
Memory Networks (LSTM) [ 11,12] to process this time-series data,
effectively emulating students’ performance in answering questions and
ultimately predicting their responses to future questions. LSTM, a type
of recurrent neural network, plays a central role in simulating student
performance within the DKT model. It employs a multitude of artificial
neurons to represent the potential knowledge state, with connections
between neurons spanning both past and future time steps to capture
the temporal dynamics of question answering. LSTM leverages the
student’s historical response data at each time step to learn the student’s
latent knowledge status and make predictions regarding their ability
to answer subsequent questions correctly. The DKT model is formally
defined as follows:

The DKT model primarily concentrates on predicting the correct-

Therefore, what distinguishes our approach from the DKT model and

insightful and comprehensive understanding of the educational context.
By automatically extracting difficulty attributes from the interaction

scenarios, contributing to a more robust and interpretable predictive
model.
2.2. Self-paced learning
Both humans and animals frequently acquire knowledge and skills
through a structured progression of problems, rather than random
arrangements [15–17]. These problems typically evolve from simpler
ones, involving fewer concepts, to more complex ones with a higher
degree of conceptual intricacy. Inspired by this learning principle, Be-
gio et al. introduced a novel learning framework known as Curriculum
Learning [18,19]. The curriculum learning training approach involves
commencing training with straightforward samples and gradually in-
troducing more challenging samples for training.
Subsequently, Kumar et al. translated this concept into a practical
technique, which they termed Self-paced Learning (SPL). SPL incorpo-
rates new regularization terms into the learning objectives, facilitating
an adaptive learning process. The SPL theory has found extensive
application in various domains, including target tracking [20], image
classification [21], and multimedia event detection [22].
2.3. Contrastive learning
Early developments in contrastive learning stemmed from self-
supervised learning, where models accomplished self-supervised learn-
ing goals through instance discrimination tasks [23]. Specifically, con-
trastive learning involves augmenting the same image differently and
assessing the similarity between the resulting pairs of images’ features.
The objective is to enhance the similarity between the augmented pairs
of the same image while reducing the similarity between features of
different images.
As technology progressed, contrastive learning expanded into su-
pervised and semi-supervised learning paradigms, leveraging annotated
data to refine models’ feature representation capabilities further. In
recent years, contrastive learning techniques within deep learning have
seen remarkable advancements. Notable methods include SimCLR [24],
MoCo v1 [25], MoCo v2 [26], BYOL [27], and others, often employing
Siamese neural network architectures. During training, image pairs are
selected either as pairs of the same image after augmentation (positive
samples) or pairs of different images after augmentation (negative
samples).
Deep contrastive learning, by conducting thorough comparisons be-
tween positive and negative sample pairs, enables neural network mod-
els to automatically extract superior feature representations from the
data. The foundational work of CPC [28] pioneered deep contrastive
learning, optimizing feature extraction networks by maximizing the
similarity between predicted and actual results of sequential data. The
introduction of the InfoNCE loss, widely adopted in current contrastive
learning research, has further strengthened these techniques.
Additionally, Khosla et al. introduced the Supervised Contrastive
Learning loss, extending the principles of contrastive learning to su-
pervised contexts for enhanced feature representation using labeled
data [29]. Meanwhile, Chen et al. devised a semi-supervised contrastive
learning algorithm, initially pretraining all data with contrastive learn-
ing and subsequently transferring the pre-trained model’s knowledge
through distillation learning using labeled data [30]. It has proven
particularly valuable in fields such as face recognition [31], speech
recognition [32,33], image classification [34,35], and other related
domains where the quantity of available data may be limited but the
diversity of categories is significant.
In educational data mining, student response data is binary and
lacks the depth and diversity inherent in image, audio, and video
data. As a result, existing contrastive learning methods do not per-
form well on educational datasets, highlighting the need for new data
enhancement methods and fine-grained rules for categorizing posi-
tive and negative examples. However, from a data-centric standpoint,
the utilization of ’one-hot’ encoding fails to encapsulate the nuanced
individual differences among students, as highlighted by previous re-
search [36]. In this paper, we adopt the concept of contrastive learning
to derive personalized encodings for individual students. Building on
the research motivation discussed earlier, the inherent susceptibility of
the original DKT model to learning local information is acknowledged,
creating a one-sided model centered solely on a student’s answer his-
tory. In this study, we address this limitation by subdividing the sample
data into global and local samples and implementing a contrastive
learning strategy to mitigate the issue of sample imbalance.

Given the intricate nature of Knowledge Tracing (KT) tasks and
the unique semantic characteristics in educational data, applying ex-
isting data augmentation methods from fields like computer vision and
natural language processing may not be suitable for student response
sequences. Therefore, we introduced a novel KT data augmentation
technique tailored specifically to the educational context. This aug-
mentation method, named Skill Replacement, is meticulously crafted to

ensure that each student’s level of proficiency in their learning history

Within the self-paced contrastive learning strategy, we incorporate a
self-paced learning technique to autonomously assess the difficulty lev-
els of current skills. When a student correctly answers a particular skill,
there is a higher likelihood of them answering skills of lower difficulty
correctly as well. Conversely, if a student answers a skill incorrectly,
they are more likely to struggle with more challenging skills. Building
on these insights, we introduce the skill replacement data augmentation
method, which maintains the student’s mastery of skills consistently,
even as their response sequence changes. In essence, it ensures that
the student’s understanding of the skills remains unchanged despite the
augmentation process.
Skill Replacement : This method adopts a knowledge structure that
captures cognitive relationships among learning materials, including
prerequisites and dependencies. To maintain simplicity, we assume
that this knowledge structure takes the form of a chain-like directed
acyclic graph, organized based on question difficulty. Specifically, we
establish a sequence of questions arranged by the probability of correct
answers, as calculated from the training data:𝑞(1),𝑞 (2), …,𝑞 (𝑀), where
𝑀 represents the total number of questions. Here, 𝑞(1) and 𝑞(𝑀)
correspond to the easiest and most challenging questions, respectively.
It is important to note that this knowledge structure can be adapted to a
more intricate form if necessary to represent the underlying educational
relationships better.
According to our motivation, we randomly select interactions with
a probability𝛾. If the student answered those interactions correctly, we
replaced the questions with easier ones. Conversely, if they answered
incorrectly, we replace them with more difficult questions. Importantly,
this process does not fundamentally change the student’s proficiency
in skills. Given the 𝑐th question 𝑞𝑡 = 𝑞(𝑐) and a positive integer 𝑘,
we can sample easier questions (𝑞(𝑐 −𝑘)) and more difficult questions
(𝑞(𝑐 + 𝑘)) based on their respective positions in the difficulty order.
This replacement method maintains the student’s overall knowledge
level while introducing variations in question difficulty to simulate real
learning scenarios.
3.4. Contrastive learning framework
The current research problem grapples with challenges associated
with sparse interactions between students and skills within online
education datasets, as illustrated in Table 1 and Fig. 1. In these datasets,
each student has responded to a substantial number of questions,
yet these questions are distributed over a very limited set of skills.
Statistical analysis reveals that some students may have answered a
thousand questions, but these questions are related to only ten skills.
When deep learning models are tasked with learning the relationship
between student responses and questions, they tend to favor students
with a larger number of responses, resulting in an excessive focus on
local information. This bias towards students with more responses may
cause the model to overlook the response patterns of students with
fewer questions, ultimately leading to inaccurate predictions of student
performance. This underscores a significant challenge in achieving
accurate knowledge tracing in scenarios characterized by such sparse
data distributions.
To mitigate the deep learning model’s overemphasis on local sam-
ples and to improve its capacity to learn from global samples, this
paper introduces an innovative contrastive learning strategy. This strat-
egy serves a dual purpose: it extracts features from student answer
sequences using contrastive learning while also striking a balance in
the model’s learning between local and global samples. To tackle the
problem of sample imbalance, the paper assigns varying weights to
local and global information. The contrastive loss function used is as
follows:

4. Experiments
4.1. Datasets
In this study, we systematically compare our approach with preva-
lent knowledge tracing methods using four publicly available educa-
tion datasets. The objective of these experiments is to predict student
performance in answering questions. Herein, we provide a concise
introduction to the public datasets employed in our analysis:
ASSISTment 2009: This dataset is sourced from the ASSISTment online
tutoring platform and is extensively utilized for Knowledge Tracing
(KT) tasks. Our experiments were conducted on the updated ’Skill
Builder’ dataset, which is known for its sparse nature. This dataset
encompasses 4151 students who answered 325,637 exercises along
with 123 distinct exercise tags.
ASSISTment 2015: This dataset, collected from the ASSISTments edu-
cational platform in 2015, differs from the 2009 dataset. It is derived
from a record of interactive sessions, with all such records being in-
cluded. During preprocessing, records with correct values other than 1
or 0 were excluded. The resulting dataset encompasses 19,840 students,
100 exercise items, and 683,801 interactions.
KDDcup2010: This dataset originates from the KDD Cup 2010 Educa-
tional Data Mining Challenge. It comprises records of interactions be-

4.2. Comparison methods
In this experiment, we compare our proposed method with the
following models, which have been chosen as representative models
in the development of knowledge tracing.
(1) DKT Model [ 5]: This model represents pioneering work in ap-
plying deep learning to knowledge tracing. It utilizes deep re-
current neural networks to process student response sequences.
The model takes each student’s response sequence as input and
predicts the probability of correctly answering the next question.
(2) DKVMN Model [ 38]: This model builds upon the DKT model and
addresses the challenge of accurately tracking specific skills mas-
tered by students. It introduces a key–value memory network,
where keys are used to store skills and values store information
about student mastery. The model continuously updates the
value network based on student response data and subsequently
predicts the probability of correctly answering future questions.
This enhancement aims to provide a more precise understanding
of individual student knowledge.
(3) SAKT Model [ 37]: This model is designed to address the issue of
sparse student interaction data for each skill in online education
datasets, which can lead to reduced prediction accuracy in neu-
ral network models. The SAKT model introduces a self-attention
supervised learning strategy. When predicting a student’s perfor-
mance on the next question, the model evaluates the similarity
between skills and historical responses. It identifies the most
similar questions as references before predicting the student’s
response. This approach aims to enhance prediction accuracy by
considering the relevance of historical responses to the current
question.
(4) ENAS-KT [ 39]: This model addresses limitations in Transform-
ers for KT by adding convolution operations to enhance local
context modeling for capturing students’ forgetting behavior. It
proposes an evolutionary neural architecture search approach to
automate input feature selection and determine optimal opera-
tions for balancing local and global context modeling. The search
space design replaces the original global path in the Transformer
with a combination of global and local paths containing differ-
ent convolutions. An effective evolutionary algorithm explores
the search space, accelerating convergence with a search space
reduction strategy.
(5) Q-MCKT [ 40]: This model addresses the issues of incorporating
individual question information into knowledge tracing and the
interpretability of prediction results by proposing the Q-MCKT
framework. It enhances the representation of less-interactive
questions through a mixture of expert techniques and a fine-
grained question-centric contrastive task. Additionally, it em-
ploys an item response theory-based prediction layer to generate
interpretable prediction results, aiming to improve the model’s
accuracy and interpretability.
Experimental Setup: During the data preprocessing phase, in-
stances with fewer than 20 student interaction records in the dataset
were omitted. This choice was guided by the reasoning that instances
with limited interaction data may suggest a lack of active engagement
from students after logging into the online learning system. Such data
were considered potential noise that could detrimentally affect the
model’s learning effectiveness, prompting their exclusion from the
experimental analysis. The deep model has three hidden layers, each
comprising 200 neurons. The entire codebase is implemented in Python

Demarcation Between Global and Local Data: The research ad-
dressed the important question of distinguishing between global and
local data within the dataset. In this experiment, using the ASSISTment
2009 dataset as an example, the following procedure was employed:
(1) The total number of student responses and skills for each student
was calculated.
(2) The ratio of skills to questions was computed, resulting in values

(3) This range was divided into 28 segments, and the number of
instances falling into each segment was recorded.
(4) A histogram was constructed based on these tallied instances as

(5) Cumulative probabilities were calculated for each segment
within the histogram.
(6) Segments with cumulative probabilities exceeding 80% were
identified as representing local information. This implies that
instances with a skill-to-question ratio greater than or equal to
0.2 would significantly influence the model’s predictions.
This method was employed to determine which instances in the dataset
should be considered as local information, thus aiding in the delin-
eation between global and local data.

5. Visualization of student knowledge state tracking results
Smooth Transition in Knowledge Tracing: The self-paced contrastive
learning strategy exhibits a smooth transition in its knowledge tracing
results. During the learning process that involves question difficulty, the
model selects samples with similar question difficulty for training. As
the student’s knowledge and ability evolve, the model gradually transi-
tions to training with more challenging question samples. This training
approach prevents a situation where most of the knowledge levels
drop simultaneously when a student answers a question incorrectly.
It ensures a more gradual and accurate representation of students’
changing knowledge states.
Variations in Student Ability Levels: Fig. 4 illustrates the fluctuations
in a student’s ability levels across three distinct skills. The first column
of the figure represents the student’s initial ability state. As the student
responds to questions associated with each skill, their ability levels for
each point undergo gradual changes. Here are some key observations
from the figure: (1) Dynamic Ability Evolution: The student’s ability
levels evolve dynamically as they answer questions. When the student
correctly answers the first question, the mastery level of the second skill
increases. Conversely, when the student answers the last four questions
incorrectly, the mastery level of the second skill starts to decline.

the mastery level of the ninth skill also decreases. (2) Identification
of Mastery: After responding to a total of 36 questions, it becomes
apparent that the student has not yet mastered the second skill. This
insight allows educators to focus further learning efforts on the second
skill until mastery is achieved. This adaptive approach to tracking
and addressing students’ knowledge gaps is a valuable aspect of the
educational data mining process.

7. Conclusion
The paper focuses on addressing the challenges of data sparsity and
sample imbalance in knowledge tracing tasks through the introduction
of a novel self-paced contrastive learning strategy. Notably, this work
represents the pioneering use of self-paced contrastive learning to im-
prove educational temporal data and handle imbalanced sample data.
The self-paced contrastive learning strategy begins by employing a self-
paced regularization term to autonomously rank question difficulty
levels. Subsequently, question replacement operations are conducted on
student response temporal sequences based on the following principle:
if a student correctly answers a question, they are likely to answer
questions of lower difficulty correctly, and if they answer a question in-
correctly, they are likely to answer more difficult questions incorrectly.
These replacements reorganize the order of questions without altering
the structure of a student’s knowledge mastery.
Moreover, traditional deep learning models tend to exhibit bias in
favor of students with larger sample sizes, as these models prioritize stu-
dents who interact with a limited number of skills in all their attempted
questions. This bias can lead to models favoring students with more
extensive data during predictions. To mitigate this issue, the paper
introduces an innovative contrastive learning strategy. This approach
first categorizes data into global and local data segments, considering
the ratio of skills involved in each student’s response sequence relative
to the total number of questions. In the novel contrastive loss function,
distinct weights are assigned to global and local data, facilitating deep
knowledge tracing models in learning from global data information.
This strategy helps balance the model’s focus between students with
varying levels of interaction with skills.
The self-paced contrastive learning strategy we have proposed has
undergone rigorous experimental validation on publicly available edu-
cational datasets, showcasing its superiority when compared to existing
knowledge tracing methods. Notably, the self-paced data augmenta-
tion component enhances the model’s interpretability by autonomously
identifying the difficulty levels of questions and skills. This not only
contributes to the model’s predictive accuracy but also provides valu-
able technical support for personalized education initiatives.
In our future work, we intend to explore alternative data augmen-
tation methods that can be effectively applied in educational contexts.
Additionally, we aim to enhance the knowledge tracing model by
considering not only the correctness of responses but also incorporating
additional online response attributes. These attributes could further
enhance the model’s predictive accuracy and provide a more com-
prehensive understanding of student performance in online learning
environments.
